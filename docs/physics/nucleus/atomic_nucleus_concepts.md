# Atomic Nucleus: In-Depth Concepts

## Overview

The **atomic nucleus** is the small, dense central core of an atom. It contains nearly all of the atom’s mass and is made primarily of two kinds of particles:

- **Protons**, which have positive electric charge.
- **Neutrons**, which have no electric charge.

Together, protons and neutrons are called **nucleons**.

The nucleus is extremely tiny compared with the atom as a whole. If an atom were scaled up to the size of a large stadium, the nucleus would be roughly the size of a small pea near the center. Most of the atom is empty space occupied by the electron cloud.

A simple picture of the atom often shows electrons orbiting a central nucleus like planets around the Sun. That picture is useful as a first sketch, but it is not physically accurate. Electrons are described by quantum orbitals, not classical circular paths. The nucleus itself is also quantum mechanical: protons and neutrons are not hard balls sitting motionless in a cluster. They are quantum particles bound together by the strong nuclear force.

---

# 1. What the nucleus contains

An atomic nucleus is made of:

\[
Z \text{ protons}
\]

and

\[
N \text{ neutrons}
\]

The total number of nucleons is called the **mass number**:

\[
A = Z + N
\]

where:

- \(Z\) = atomic number = number of protons.
- \(N\) = neutron number = number of neutrons.
- \(A\) = mass number = total number of protons and neutrons.

The number of protons determines the element.

For example:

| Element | Protons \(Z\) |
|---|---:|
| Hydrogen | 1 |
| Helium | 2 |
| Carbon | 6 |
| Oxygen | 8 |
| Iron | 26 |
| Uranium | 92 |

Changing the number of protons changes the element. Changing the number of neutrons creates a different **isotope** of the same element.

---

# 2. Isotopes

**Isotopes** are atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons.

For example, carbon always has 6 protons. But carbon can have different numbers of neutrons:

| Isotope | Protons | Neutrons | Mass number |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Carbon-12 | 6 | 6 | 12 |
| Carbon-13 | 6 | 7 | 13 |
| Carbon-14 | 6 | 8 | 14 |

Carbon-12 and carbon-13 are stable. Carbon-14 is radioactive.

The notation for an isotope is often written as:

\[
^{A}_{Z}X
\]

where:

- \(X\) is the chemical symbol.
- \(A\) is the mass number.
- \(Z\) is the atomic number.

For carbon-14:

\[
^{14}_{6}\text{C}
\]

This means carbon with 6 protons and 14 total nucleons, so it has:

\[
N = A - Z = 14 - 6 = 8
\]

neutrons.

---

# 3. Size of the nucleus

Nuclei are measured in **femtometers**.

\[
1 \text{ femtometer} = 10^{-15} \text{ meters}
\]

A typical nuclear radius is modeled approximately by:

\[
R = R_0 A^{1/3}
\]

where:

\[
R_0 \approx 1.2 \text{ fm}
\]

and \(A\) is the mass number.

This equation means that nuclear radius grows with the cube root of the number of nucleons. The cube-root dependence makes sense because nuclear volume is roughly proportional to the number of nucleons:

\[
V \propto R^3
\]

If:

\[
R \propto A^{1/3}
\]

then:

\[
V \propto A
\]

So adding more nucleons increases the volume roughly linearly.

This also means nuclear density is approximately constant across many nuclei. A large nucleus is not much denser than a small nucleus; it is mostly just bigger.

---

# 4. Nuclear density

The nucleus is incredibly dense.

A rough nuclear density is about:

\[
\rho \sim 2 \times 10^{17} \text{ kg/m}^3
\]

This is far denser than ordinary matter.

The reason ordinary matter is not that dense is that atoms are mostly empty space. The electrons occupy a region far larger than the nucleus.

A useful comparison:

- Atomic radius: about \(10^{-10}\) meters.
- Nuclear radius: about \(10^{-15}\) meters.

The atom is about:

\[
10^5
\]

or 100,000 times wider than the nucleus.

Since volume scales as radius cubed, the atom’s volume is roughly:

\[
(10^5)^3 = 10^{15}
\]

or one quadrillion times larger than the nucleus.

---

# 5. Protons

A **proton** is a positively charged nucleon.

Its charge is:

\[
+e
\]

where:

\[
e \approx 1.602 \times 10^{-19} \text{ C}
\]

A proton’s mass is approximately:

\[
1.6726 \times 10^{-27} \text{ kg}
\]

In particle physics, the proton is not fundamental. It is made of smaller particles called **quarks**.

A proton contains:

\[
uud
\]

meaning:

- 2 up quarks.
- 1 down quark.

The charges of these quarks are:

\[
\text{up quark} = +\frac{2}{3}e
\]

\[
\text{down quark} = -\frac{1}{3}e
\]

So the proton’s total charge is:

\[
+\frac{2}{3}e + \frac{2}{3}e - \frac{1}{3}e = +e
\]

---

# 6. Neutrons

A **neutron** is an electrically neutral nucleon.

Its mass is approximately:

\[
1.6749 \times 10^{-27} \text{ kg}
\]

It is slightly heavier than a proton.

A neutron is also made of quarks. It contains:

\[
udd
\]

meaning:

- 1 up quark.
- 2 down quarks.

Its total charge is:

\[
+\frac{2}{3}e - \frac{1}{3}e - \frac{1}{3}e = 0
\]

A free neutron is unstable. Outside the nucleus, it undergoes beta decay with a lifetime of about 15 minutes on average. Inside many nuclei, neutrons can be stable because the nuclear environment changes the energy balance.

---

# 7. Why the nucleus does not fly apart

A nucleus contains positively charged protons. Since like charges repel, every proton electrically repels every other proton.

The electric repulsion between two protons is described by Coulomb’s law:

\[
F_E = k_e \frac{e^2}{r^2}
\]

This repulsion is significant inside the nucleus because protons are extremely close together.

So why does the nucleus stay together?

The answer is the **strong nuclear force**.

The strong nuclear force is attractive between nearby nucleons and is much stronger than electric repulsion at very short distances. It binds protons and neutrons together into a nucleus.

However, the strong nuclear force has a very short range. It becomes weak beyond distances of a few femtometers.

This creates a competition:

- The strong nuclear force pulls nearby nucleons together.
- The electromagnetic force pushes protons apart.
- The weak nuclear force allows certain types of radioactive decay.
- Quantum mechanics determines the allowed nuclear energy states.

---

# 8. The strong nuclear force

The strong nuclear force has two related meanings:

1. The fundamental strong interaction between quarks and gluons.
2. The residual strong force between protons and neutrons inside nuclei.

The fundamental strong force is described by **quantum chromodynamics**, or **QCD**. It binds quarks together inside protons and neutrons.

The nuclear force between nucleons is a leftover effect of the deeper quark-gluon interaction. It is somewhat similar to how neutral atoms can still attract each other through residual electromagnetic effects.

Important features of the nuclear force:

- It is very strong at distances around 1 femtometer.
- It is short-ranged.
- It is attractive at typical nucleon separations.
- It becomes repulsive at extremely short distances.
- It acts between protons and protons, neutrons and neutrons, and protons and neutrons.

The short-range repulsion helps prevent nucleons from collapsing into a single point.

---

# 9. Binding energy

When protons and neutrons bind together to form a nucleus, energy is released. This means the mass of the final nucleus is less than the total mass of the separate protons and neutrons.

This missing mass is called the **mass defect**.

Einstein’s equation relates mass and energy:

\[
E = mc^2
\]

The nuclear binding energy is:

\[
E_b = \Delta m c^2
\]

where \(\Delta m\) is the mass defect.

If:

\[
\Delta m = Zm_p + Nm_n - m_{\text{nucleus}}
\]

then:

\[
E_b = \left(Zm_p + Nm_n - m_{\text{nucleus}}\right)c^2
\]

Binding energy is the energy required to completely separate a nucleus into individual protons and neutrons.

A tightly bound nucleus has a large binding energy.

---

# 10. Binding energy per nucleon

The **binding energy per nucleon** is:

\[
\frac{E_b}{A}
\]

This tells us how strongly, on average, each nucleon is bound inside the nucleus.

The binding energy per nucleon increases rapidly for small nuclei, reaches a maximum near iron and nickel, and then slowly decreases for very heavy nuclei.

This explains why:

- Light nuclei can release energy by **fusion**.
- Heavy nuclei can release energy by **fission**.

Fusion combines light nuclei into heavier nuclei. This moves them toward higher binding energy per nucleon.

Fission splits very heavy nuclei into smaller nuclei. This also moves the products toward higher binding energy per nucleon.

In both cases, the final products are more tightly bound, so energy is released.

---

# 11. The semi-empirical mass formula

Nuclear binding energies can be approximated using the **semi-empirical mass formula**, also called the liquid-drop formula.

It treats the nucleus somewhat like a charged liquid drop.

A simplified form is:

\[
B(A,Z) =
 a_v A
- a_s A^{2/3}
- a_c \frac{Z(Z-1)}{A^{1/3}}
- a_a \frac{(A-2Z)^2}{A}
+ \delta(A,Z)
\]

The terms represent different physical effects:

## Volume term

\[
a_v A
\]

Each nucleon attracts nearby nucleons, so binding roughly increases with the number of nucleons.

## Surface term

\[
-a_s A^{2/3}
\]

Nucleons on the surface have fewer neighbors, so they are less tightly bound.

## Coulomb term

\[
-a_c \frac{Z(Z-1)}{A^{1/3}}
\]

Protons repel each other electrically, reducing binding energy.

## Asymmetry term

\[
-a_a \frac{(A-2Z)^2}{A}
\]

Nuclei are more stable when proton and neutron numbers are balanced, especially for light elements. For heavier elements, extra neutrons are needed to offset proton-proton repulsion.

## Pairing term

\[
\delta(A,Z)
\]

Nuclei with paired protons and paired neutrons tend to be more stable.

---

# 12. Why neutrons are needed

A nucleus with many protons needs neutrons for stability.

Neutrons help in two major ways:

1. They add attractive strong-force interactions.
2. They do not add electric repulsion.

For light nuclei, stable isotopes often have approximately equal numbers of protons and neutrons:

\[
N \approx Z
\]

For heavier nuclei, stable isotopes usually require more neutrons than protons:

\[
N > Z
\]

This is because proton-proton electric repulsion grows as the nucleus gets larger. Extra neutrons increase strong-force binding without increasing electric repulsion.

---

# 13. Nuclear stability

A nucleus is stable if it is energetically favorable for it to remain as it is.

An unstable nucleus can transform through radioactive decay.

Stability depends on several factors:

- Proton number.
- Neutron number.
- Binding energy.
- Available decay paths.
- Quantum energy levels.
- Pairing of nucleons.
- Coulomb repulsion.

There is a region of stable nuclei called the **band of stability**.

Light stable nuclei tend to lie near:

\[
N = Z
\]

Heavy stable nuclei lie above that line, with more neutrons than protons.

Very heavy nuclei become unstable because electric repulsion among many protons becomes too large for the short-range nuclear force to fully overcome.

---

# 14. Radioactive decay

**Radioactive decay** happens when an unstable nucleus transforms into a more stable configuration.

Common types include:

- Alpha decay.
- Beta-minus decay.
- Beta-plus decay.
- Electron capture.
- Gamma decay.
- Spontaneous fission.

Radioactive decay is probabilistic. You cannot predict exactly when a single unstable nucleus will decay, but you can predict the statistical behavior of a large number of nuclei.

---

# 15. Half-life

The **half-life** of a radioactive isotope is the time it takes for half of a sample to decay.

If a sample starts with \(N_0\) unstable nuclei, the number remaining after time \(t\) is:

\[
N(t) = N_0 e^{-\lambda t}
\]

where \(\lambda\) is the decay constant.

The half-life \(t_{1/2}\) is related to \(\lambda\) by:

\[
t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\lambda}
\]

After one half-life, half remains.

After two half-lives, one fourth remains.

After three half-lives, one eighth remains.

This exponential behavior comes from the fact that each nucleus has a constant probability per unit time of decaying.

---

# 16. Alpha decay

In **alpha decay**, a nucleus emits an alpha particle.

An alpha particle is a helium-4 nucleus:

\[
^{4}_{2}\text{He}
\]

It contains:

- 2 protons.
- 2 neutrons.

A general alpha decay looks like:

\[
^{A}_{Z}X \rightarrow ^{A-4}_{Z-2}Y + ^4_2\text{He}
\]

The mass number decreases by 4, and the atomic number decreases by 2.

Example:

\[
^{238}_{92}\text{U} \rightarrow ^{234}_{90}\text{Th} + ^4_2\text{He}
\]

Alpha decay often occurs in heavy nuclei where reducing size and proton repulsion leads to a more stable configuration.

Quantum tunneling is essential to alpha decay. The alpha particle is trapped by the nuclear potential but has a probability of tunneling through the energy barrier.

---

# 17. Beta-minus decay

In **beta-minus decay**, a neutron turns into a proton, emitting an electron and an antineutrino.

At the nucleon level:

\[
n \rightarrow p + e^- + \bar{\nu}_e
\]

Inside a nucleus:

\[
^{A}_{Z}X \rightarrow ^A_{Z+1}Y + e^- + \bar{\nu}_e
\]

The mass number stays the same, but the atomic number increases by 1.

Beta-minus decay occurs when a nucleus has too many neutrons relative to protons.

It is governed by the weak nuclear interaction.

---

# 18. Beta-plus decay

In **beta-plus decay**, a proton turns into a neutron, emitting a positron and a neutrino.

At the nucleon level:

\[
p \rightarrow n + e^+ + \nu_e
\]

Inside a nucleus:

\[
^{A}_{Z}X \rightarrow ^A_{Z-1}Y + e^+ + \nu_e
\]

The mass number stays the same, but the atomic number decreases by 1.

Beta-plus decay occurs when a nucleus has too many protons relative to neutrons.

A positron is the antimatter counterpart of the electron.

---

# 19. Electron capture

In **electron capture**, a proton in the nucleus captures an inner electron and becomes a neutron.

\[
p + e^- \rightarrow n + \nu_e
\]

Inside a nucleus:

\[
^{A}_{Z}X + e^- \rightarrow ^A_{Z-1}Y + \nu_e
\]

This also lowers the atomic number by 1 while keeping the mass number the same.

Electron capture competes with beta-plus decay in some proton-rich nuclei.

---

# 20. Gamma decay

In **gamma decay**, an excited nucleus releases energy by emitting a gamma-ray photon.

\[
^{A}_{Z}X^* \rightarrow ^A_ZX + \gamma
\]

The asterisk indicates an excited nuclear state.

Gamma decay does not change the number of protons or neutrons. It only lowers the energy of the nucleus.

Gamma rays are high-energy electromagnetic radiation.

---

# 21. Nuclear energy levels

Like electrons in atoms, nuclei have quantized energy levels.

A nucleus can exist in a ground state or in excited states.

When a nucleus transitions from a higher energy state to a lower energy state, it can emit a gamma ray.

The energy of the gamma photon is:

\[
E_\gamma = E_i - E_f
\]

where:

- \(E_i\) is the initial nuclear energy level.
- \(E_f\) is the final nuclear energy level.

Nuclear energy levels are usually much more energetic than electronic energy levels. Electronic transitions often involve electronvolts, while nuclear transitions often involve kiloelectronvolts or megaelectronvolts.

---

# 22. The shell model of the nucleus

The **nuclear shell model** describes protons and neutrons as occupying quantized energy levels inside the nucleus.

This is somewhat analogous to how electrons occupy orbitals in atoms, but the force and details are different.

In the shell model:

- Protons occupy proton energy levels.
- Neutrons occupy neutron energy levels.
- The Pauli exclusion principle applies.
- Filled shells create especially stable nuclei.

Certain numbers of protons or neutrons produce extra stability. These are called **magic numbers**.

Common magic numbers are:

\[
2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82, 126
\]

A nucleus with a magic number of protons or neutrons is more stable than nearby nuclei.

A **doubly magic** nucleus has magic numbers of both protons and neutrons.

Examples include:

- Helium-4: 2 protons, 2 neutrons.
- Oxygen-16: 8 protons, 8 neutrons.
- Calcium-40: 20 protons, 20 neutrons.
- Lead-208: 82 protons, 126 neutrons.

---

# 23. The liquid-drop model

The **liquid-drop model** treats the nucleus like a tiny drop of incompressible nuclear fluid.

This model is useful for explaining:

- Approximate nuclear sizes.
- Binding energy trends.
- Surface effects.
- Coulomb repulsion.
- Nuclear fission.

In this model, nucleons interact mostly with nearby neighbors, similar to molecules in a liquid drop.

The model explains why binding energy has volume and surface terms. Interior nucleons are surrounded by many neighbors and are strongly bound. Surface nucleons have fewer neighbors and contribute less binding.

However, the liquid-drop model does not explain all nuclear properties. It misses many quantum shell effects.

---

# 24. The collective model

The **collective model** combines ideas from the shell model and liquid-drop model.

It describes the nucleus as having both:

- Individual nucleon motion.
- Collective motion of the whole nucleus.

Nuclei can rotate, vibrate, and deform.

Some nuclei are nearly spherical. Others are shaped more like ellipsoids.

Common deformation types include:

- Prolate: stretched like a football.
- Oblate: flattened like a disk.
- Triaxial: unequal axes in three directions.

Nuclear deformation affects energy levels and gamma-ray spectra.

---

# 25. Nuclear spin

Nucleons have intrinsic angular momentum called **spin**.

Protons and neutrons each have spin:

\[
\frac{1}{2}
\]

Nucleons can also have orbital angular momentum due to their motion inside the nucleus.

The total nuclear spin comes from combining:

- Proton spins.
- Neutron spins.
- Orbital angular momenta.

Many even-even nuclei, meaning nuclei with even numbers of protons and even numbers of neutrons, have total spin 0 in their ground state. This happens because nucleons tend to pair with opposite angular momenta.

Odd-A nuclei often have spin determined largely by the unpaired nucleon.

---

# 26. Nuclear magnetic moment

Because protons and neutrons have spin and internal charge structure, nuclei can have magnetic moments.

A nuclear magnetic moment is important in technologies such as **nuclear magnetic resonance**, or **NMR**.

MRI machines use the magnetic properties of nuclei, especially hydrogen nuclei, to create images of the body.

Hydrogen is useful because its nucleus is a single proton, and the human body contains many hydrogen atoms in water and organic molecules.

---

# 27. Nuclear force versus electromagnetic force

Inside the nucleus, two major forces compete:

## Strong nuclear force

- Attractive at typical nucleon distances.
- Very short-ranged.
- Acts between nucleons.
- Stronger than electromagnetic repulsion at nuclear distances.

## Electromagnetic force

- Repulsive between protons.
- Long-ranged.
- Gets stronger with more protons.
- Makes very heavy nuclei unstable.

The strong force has a saturation property: each nucleon mainly binds to nearby nucleons, not equally to all nucleons in the nucleus.

The electric force does not saturate in the same way. Every proton repels every other proton, even across the entire nucleus.

This is one reason extremely large nuclei are unstable.

---

# 28. Fission

**Nuclear fission** is the splitting of a heavy nucleus into smaller nuclei.

A typical fission reaction releases:

- Smaller daughter nuclei.
- Free neutrons.
- Gamma rays.
- Kinetic energy.

Example of uranium-235 fission:

\[
^{235}_{92}\text{U} + n \rightarrow ^{236}_{92}\text{U}^* \rightarrow \text{fission fragments} + \text{neutrons} + \text{energy}
\]

The excited uranium-236 nucleus can deform and split.

Fission releases energy because the daughter nuclei are more tightly bound per nucleon than the original heavy nucleus.

---

# 29. Chain reactions

In some fission reactions, emitted neutrons can cause additional fissions.

This creates a **chain reaction**.

For a sustained chain reaction, on average at least one neutron from each fission must cause another fission.

The multiplication factor is often called \(k\):

- \(k < 1\): subcritical; reaction dies out.
- \(k = 1\): critical; reaction is steady.
- \(k > 1\): supercritical; reaction grows.

Nuclear reactors are designed to maintain a controlled critical chain reaction.

---

# 30. Fusion

**Nuclear fusion** is the joining of light nuclei into a heavier nucleus.

Fusion powers stars.

In the Sun, hydrogen nuclei ultimately combine to form helium through a sequence of reactions called the proton-proton chain.

A simplified overall reaction is:

\[
4p \rightarrow ^4_2\text{He} + 2e^+ + 2\nu_e + \text{energy}
\]

Fusion releases energy because helium-4 is more tightly bound than four separate protons.

The challenge is that nuclei are positively charged, so they repel each other electrically. They must get extremely close for the strong nuclear force to bind them.

This requires very high temperatures and pressures.

Quantum tunneling helps fusion occur in stars. Even when particles do not classically have enough energy to overcome the Coulomb barrier, there is a probability that they can tunnel through it.

---

# 31. Coulomb barrier

The **Coulomb barrier** is the electric repulsion that two positively charged nuclei must overcome to fuse.

The approximate electric potential energy between two nuclei is:

\[
U = k_e \frac{Z_1 Z_2 e^2}{r}
\]

where:

- \(Z_1\) and \(Z_2\) are the proton numbers of the nuclei.
- \(r\) is the distance between them.

At large distances, the nuclei repel. At very short distances, the strong nuclear force becomes dominant and can bind them.

Fusion requires nuclei to approach close enough for the strong force to act.

---

# 32. Quantum tunneling in nuclear physics

Quantum tunneling is when a particle has a probability of crossing a barrier that it could not cross in classical physics.

Tunneling is important in:

- Alpha decay.
- Nuclear fusion in stars.
- Some nuclear reaction rates.

In alpha decay, an alpha particle is trapped inside the nuclear potential but can tunnel out.

In stellar fusion, two nuclei can tunnel through part of the Coulomb barrier, allowing fusion at temperatures lower than classical physics would require.

---

# 33. The nucleus as a quantum system

The nucleus is not a miniature solar system.

It is a many-body quantum system.

This means:

- Nucleons do not have perfectly defined classical paths.
- Energy levels are quantized.
- The Pauli exclusion principle matters.
- Spin and angular momentum are quantized.
- Particles can tunnel.
- The nucleus can exist in superpositions of states.

A complete description of a nucleus requires quantum mechanics and the nuclear interaction.

Because nuclei contain many strongly interacting particles, exact calculations are extremely difficult.

---

# 34. The Pauli exclusion principle in nuclei

Protons and neutrons are fermions.

Fermions obey the **Pauli exclusion principle**: no two identical fermions can occupy the exact same quantum state.

Protons and neutrons are treated as different particles for nuclear shell filling.

This means:

- Two protons cannot have identical quantum numbers.
- Two neutrons cannot have identical quantum numbers.
- A proton and neutron can occupy similar spatial states because they are not identical particles.

The Pauli exclusion principle helps create nuclear shell structure.

---

# 35. Pairing effects

Nucleons tend to form pairs with opposite spins and angular momenta.

This pairing lowers the energy of the nucleus.

As a result:

- Even-even nuclei are often especially stable.
- Odd-odd nuclei are often less stable.
- Nuclei with paired protons and paired neutrons have higher binding energies.

This is why the pairing term appears in the semi-empirical mass formula.

---

# 36. Nuclear reactions

A **nuclear reaction** changes the composition or energy state of a nucleus.

General form:

\[
a + X \rightarrow Y + b
\]

where a projectile \(a\) hits a target nucleus \(X\), producing a new nucleus \(Y\) and outgoing particle \(b\).

Examples include:

- Neutron capture.
- Proton capture.
- Alpha capture.
- Fission.
- Fusion.
- Spallation.

Nuclear reactions must conserve:

- Energy.
- Momentum.
- Electric charge.
- Baryon number.
- Lepton number, when leptons are involved.
- Angular momentum.

---

# 37. Neutron capture

In **neutron capture**, a nucleus absorbs a neutron:

\[
^A_ZX + n \rightarrow ^{A+1}_ZX^*
\]

The resulting nucleus may be excited and emit gamma radiation:

\[
^{A+1}_ZX^* \rightarrow ^{A+1}_ZX + \gamma
\]

Neutron capture is important in:

- Nuclear reactors.
- Formation of heavy elements in stars.
- Radioactive isotope production.

Because neutrons are electrically neutral, they are not repelled by the Coulomb barrier. This makes neutron capture easier than charged-particle capture in many situations.

---

# 38. Nucleosynthesis

**Nucleosynthesis** is the creation of atomic nuclei.

Major nucleosynthesis processes include:

## Big Bang nucleosynthesis

Produced mostly:

- Hydrogen.
- Helium.
- Small amounts of deuterium.
- Small amounts of lithium.

## Stellar nucleosynthesis

Stars fuse light nuclei into heavier nuclei.

Massive stars can produce elements up to iron and nickel through fusion stages.

## Supernova nucleosynthesis

Explosive stellar events produce and distribute heavy elements.

## Neutron-capture processes

Heavy elements can form by neutron capture.

Two major types are:

- The s-process, or slow neutron capture.
- The r-process, or rapid neutron capture.

The r-process is associated with extremely neutron-rich environments, such as neutron star mergers and certain supernova-related environments.

---

# 39. Why iron is special

Iron and nickel are near the peak of binding energy per nucleon.

This means they are among the most tightly bound nuclei.

For nuclei lighter than iron, fusion generally releases energy.

For nuclei heavier than iron, fission can release energy.

Fusion beyond iron usually requires energy instead of releasing it.

This is why ordinary stellar fusion cannot keep extracting energy indefinitely by making heavier and heavier elements.

When a massive star builds up an iron-rich core, fusion no longer provides enough outward pressure to support the star, leading to core collapse under the right conditions.

---

# 40. Mass-energy conversion

Nuclear reactions release far more energy per atom than chemical reactions.

Chemical reactions rearrange electrons and usually involve energies of electronvolts per atom.

Nuclear reactions rearrange nucleons and can involve megaelectronvolts per nucleus.

The energy comes from changes in nuclear binding energy.

If the final products have less total mass than the initial particles, the missing mass appears as released energy:

\[
E = \Delta m c^2
\]

Even a tiny mass difference corresponds to a large energy because \(c^2\) is enormous.

---

# 41. Units in nuclear physics

Nuclear physics often uses the **electronvolt**.

\[
1 \text{ eV} = 1.602 \times 10^{-19} \text{ J}
\]

Common units include:

\[
1 \text{ keV} = 10^3 \text{ eV}
\]

\[
1 \text{ MeV} = 10^6 \text{ eV}
\]

\[
1 \text{ GeV} = 10^9 \text{ eV}
\]

Nuclear binding energies are often measured in MeV.

The mass of a proton is about:

\[
938 \text{ MeV}/c^2
\]

The mass of a neutron is about:

\[
939 \text{ MeV}/c^2
\]

---

# 42. Atomic mass unit

The **atomic mass unit**, or unified atomic mass unit, is useful for nuclear masses.

\[
1 \text{ u} \approx 1.6605 \times 10^{-27} \text{ kg}
\]

In energy units:

\[
1 \text{ u} \approx 931.5 \text{ MeV}/c^2
\]

This conversion is useful for calculating nuclear binding energy from mass defects.

If the mass defect is \(0.01\text{ u}\), the energy equivalent is approximately:

\[
0.01 \times 931.5 \text{ MeV} = 9.315 \text{ MeV}
\]

---

# 43. Example: helium-4 binding energy

A helium-4 nucleus contains:

- 2 protons.
- 2 neutrons.

If the mass of the separated nucleons is greater than the mass of the helium nucleus, the difference is binding energy.

The binding energy of helium-4 is about 28.3 MeV total.

Binding energy per nucleon is therefore:

\[
\frac{28.3\text{ MeV}}{4} \approx 7.1\text{ MeV per nucleon}
\]

Helium-4 is especially stable because it has 2 protons and 2 neutrons, corresponding to filled nuclear shells.

---

# 44. Example: carbon-12 nucleus

Carbon-12 has:

\[
Z = 6
\]

\[
N = 6
\]

\[
A = 12
\]

It is stable and very important for life.

Carbon-12 can be thought of in different nuclear models:

- In the shell model, protons and neutrons fill quantum levels.
- In cluster models, carbon-12 can be approximated as three alpha-like clusters in some excited-state descriptions.
- In the liquid-drop model, it is a small nuclear droplet.

No single model captures every feature perfectly.

---

# 45. Example: uranium-238 nucleus

Uranium-238 has:

\[
Z = 92
\]

\[
A = 238
\]

So:

\[
N = 238 - 92 = 146
\]

It has many more neutrons than protons.

This neutron excess helps reduce the destabilizing effect of proton-proton repulsion, but uranium-238 is still radioactive.

It decays mainly by alpha decay:

\[
^{238}_{92}\text{U} \rightarrow ^{234}_{90}\text{Th} + ^4_2\text{He}
\]

Very heavy nuclei tend to be unstable because the long-range Coulomb repulsion among protons becomes too large.

---

# 46. Nuclear potential well

A useful model treats nucleons as moving inside a nuclear potential well.

The strong force creates an attractive well that keeps nucleons bound.

A simplified one-dimensional picture has:

- Low potential inside the nucleus.
- High potential outside the nucleus.
- Quantized bound states inside the well.

For protons, the Coulomb repulsion adds an electric barrier outside the nucleus.

For neutrons, there is no Coulomb barrier because they are electrically neutral.

This kind of model helps explain bound states, tunneling, and decay.

---

# 47. Alpha decay and the potential barrier

In alpha decay, the alpha particle can be thought of as trapped inside the nucleus.

Classically, if the alpha particle does not have enough energy to climb over the Coulomb barrier, it should remain trapped forever.

Quantum mechanically, the alpha particle has a probability of tunneling through the barrier.

This explains why alpha decay can happen even when classical physics says it should not.

The tunneling probability depends strongly on:

- Barrier height.
- Barrier width.
- Alpha particle energy.

Small changes in energy can produce huge changes in half-life.

---

# 48. The nuclear chart

The **chart of nuclides** arranges nuclei by proton number and neutron number.

Usually:

- Proton number \(Z\) is on one axis.
- Neutron number \(N\) is on the other axis.

Stable nuclei form a curved band.

Nuclei away from this band are unstable and decay toward stability through processes such as beta decay, alpha decay, or fission.

The chart of nuclides is like a map of nuclear stability.

# 49. Common misconceptions

## Misconception: protons and neutrons are tiny solid balls

They are not solid classical balls. They are quantum particles with internal quark-gluon structure.

## Misconception: the nucleus is static

The nucleus is dynamic. Nucleons move in quantum states, and nuclei can vibrate, rotate, deform, and transition between energy levels.

## Misconception: neutrons are just glue

Neutrons do help stabilize nuclei, but they are not passive glue. They are quantum particles with their own shell structure, spin, and interactions.

## Misconception: nuclear energy comes from destroying matter

Nuclear energy usually comes from changes in binding energy. A small amount of mass-energy is converted into kinetic energy, radiation, and other forms of energy.

## Misconception: radioactive decay is predictable for one atom

Decay is probabilistic. You can predict the average behavior of many atoms, but not the exact decay time of a specific nucleus.

# 50. Building a simple nucleus model for a simulation

A simulation can represent the nucleus at different levels of realism.

## Level 1: visual particle model

Represent protons and neutrons as colored particles packed into a small region.

Useful features:

- Protons repel each other electrically.
- Nucleons attract each other at short range.
- Very short-range repulsion prevents overlap.
- Stable clusters form under the right conditions.

This model is intuitive and visually useful, but not fully quantum mechanical.

## Level 2: force-based classical model

Use approximate forces:

\[
F_{\text{strong}}(r)
\]

for short-range attraction and repulsion, plus Coulomb repulsion between protons:

\[
F_{\text{Coulomb}} = k \frac{q_1 q_2}{r^2}
\]

This can show why nuclei need neutrons and why heavy nuclei become unstable.

## Level 3: binding-energy model

Use equations such as the semi-empirical mass formula to estimate stability and energy release.

This is good for modeling:

- Isotopes.
- Binding energy.
- Fission energy.
- Fusion energy.
- Stability trends.

## Level 4: quantum shell model

Represent nucleons as filling discrete quantum states.

This can show:

- Magic numbers.
- Pairing effects.
- Nuclear spin patterns.
- Shell closures.

This is more realistic conceptually but harder to visualize.

---

# 51. A simple conceptual simulation rule set

For a visually understandable nucleus simulator, you can use rules like:

1. Protons repel all other protons through a Coulomb-like force.
2. All nucleons attract nearby nucleons through a short-range nuclear force.
3. The nuclear attraction falls off quickly beyond a few femtometers.
4. At extremely small distances, nucleons repel strongly to avoid collapse.
5. Neutrons increase binding without adding electric repulsion.
6. If total binding is too weak, particles can escape.
7. If the nucleus is too neutron-rich or proton-rich, beta decay may become likely.
8. If the nucleus is very heavy, alpha decay or fission may become possible.

This kind of simulation will not be exact nuclear physics, but it can capture the main qualitative ideas.

---

# 52. Suggested visual elements

A nucleus simulation can show:

- Protons as red or positive particles.
- Neutrons as gray or neutral particles.
- Electric repulsion arrows between protons.
- Short-range attraction between nearby nucleons.
- A radius boundary based on \(R = R_0A^{1/3}\).
- Binding energy per nucleon.
- Isotope identity.
- Stability indicator.
- Decay mode prediction.
- Magic number highlights.

For example, when the user changes neutron number, the simulator could show whether the isotope is near the band of stability.

# 53. Mathematical model for a simple force simulation

A simplified pair potential between nucleons could have this shape:

\[
V(r) = V_{\text{rep}}(r) + V_{\text{attr}}(r) + V_{\text{coulomb}}(r)
\]

The short-range repulsive part prevents collapse.

The medium-short-range attractive part binds nucleons.

The Coulomb part applies only between protons.

A possible qualitative form is:

\[
V_{\text{nuclear}}(r) = A e^{-r/r_1} - B e^{-r/r_2}
\]

where:

- The first term can represent short-range repulsion.
- The second term can represent attraction.
- \(A\), \(B\), \(r_1\), and \(r_2\) are adjustable constants.

For proton-proton interactions, add:

\[
V_{\text{coulomb}}(r) = k \frac{e^2}{r}
\]

This is not a complete physical nuclear potential, but it can produce useful educational behavior.

---

# 54. Limitations of classical nucleus simulations

A classical particle simulation of the nucleus is educational, but it has limits.

Real nuclei require quantum mechanics.

Important effects that a simple classical model may miss include:

- Pauli exclusion.
- Shell structure.
- Spin-orbit coupling.
- Quantum tunneling.
- Wavefunction antisymmetry.
- Pairing correlations.
- Nuclear deformation from quantum collective motion.
- Exact decay probabilities.

A good educational simulation should clearly state whether it is showing a physical calculation or a conceptual visualization.

---

# 55. The core picture

The atomic nucleus is a tiny, dense, quantum-mechanical system made of protons and neutrons.

Protons repel each other electrically, but the strong nuclear force binds nearby nucleons together.

Neutrons are essential because they add strong-force attraction without adding electric repulsion.

The stability of a nucleus depends on the balance among nuclear attraction, electromagnetic repulsion, quantum shell structure, pairing, and available decay paths.

Binding energy explains why some nuclear reactions release energy. Fusion releases energy for light nuclei because the products are more tightly bound. Fission releases energy for heavy nuclei because the fragments are more tightly bound.

A simple visual model can represent the nucleus as a cluster of protons and neutrons with short-range attraction and proton-proton repulsion, but a truly accurate model requires quantum mechanics.
